The Better Wrap

Why ML-KEM Changes What "Protected" Means

Many real-world encryption designs have one recurring problem: the data can stay protected with a strong symmetric cipher like AES-256-GCM, but the AES key itself still has to move between systems, business partners, services, or trust boundaries. That is where key wrapping enters the picture. In practice, this is often part of what teams call envelope encryption.

Anytime an AES key is created in one location and must be safely delivered somewhere else, it needs its own layer of protection. That happens in common business scenarios such as a company sharing protected payloads with a supplier, a platform encrypting content for another service tier, a data pipeline handing off encrypted records between systems, or an application wrapping a content key for later use by a different trust domain.

In those designs, AES protects the content, but a public-key system protects the AES key. For years, RSA performed that role. ML-KEM is the post-quantum answer for the same class of use cases: it protects the symmetric key that protects the valuable content.

Think of this protection like packing lunch. The inside of the sandwich is the valuable part. The wrap decides whether it arrives intact, private, and untouched. For years, RSA handled the wrap. ML-KEM is the new post-quantum wrap built for a world where attackers do not just steal lunch today. They can record wrapped keys and encrypted traffic now, then wait for better tools tomorrow.

That is the heart of the post-quantum migration story. We are not replacing AES-256-GCM because it is weak. We are replacing the public-key mechanism that protects or establishes the AES key. Traditional RSA wrapping is still common, but it carries a long-term quantum risk that ML-KEM was standardized to address.

The "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later" Problem Is the Real Business Story

The urgency around ML-KEM is not that RSA suddenly fails on ordinary computers. The issue is that organizations protect data with public-key mechanisms whose long-term safety can change dramatically if cryptographically relevant quantum computers become practical.

What an attacker can do today

  • Capture encrypted traffic or stored encrypted payloads.
  • Collect wrapped AES keys, key exchanges, and certificate chains.
  • Wait years for stronger cryptanalytic capability.

Why that matters tomorrow

  • RSA depends on math that a sufficiently capable quantum computer could attack efficiently with Shor's algorithm.
  • If the legacy key transport is broken later, archived data can become readable later.
  • ML-KEM is designed as a post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism to reduce that long-term exposure.
What changes with ML-KEM? RSA usually generates an AES key first and then wraps it. ML-KEM encapsulates to a public key and derives the AES key from the shared secret. Same payload protection. Different trust model for the key establishment step.

There is also a deeper reason organizations are moving this direction. RSA is built on a math problem that works well against normal computers, but it is one of the problem types large quantum computers are expected to attack much more effectively. That is why teams worry about "harvest now, decrypt later": even if the protected payload cannot be opened today, the wrapped key material may still be worth stealing and storing for later.

ML-KEM was selected for the opposite reason. It uses a different family of mathematics designed for the post-quantum era, so it does not carry the same factoring-based weakness as legacy RSA. In business terms, ML-KEM is not just a new wrapper. It is a safer long-horizon way to protect the AES keys that unlock your most valuable data.

The most valuable part of tomorrow's breach may be data an attacker quietly collected years earlier.

Page 3 - Key Custody

How You Store the Private Key Matters Almost as Much as Which Algorithm You Choose

Algorithm choice and key custody are different decisions, but they are deeply linked. A post-quantum migration that keeps weak key custody habits is only a partial win. The security conversation quickly moves from what algorithm? to where does the private key live?

Software convenience

Flat files, PEM bundles, or software keystores are easy to deploy and easy to reason about. They are also easier to copy, harder to inventory, and harder to defend in audit discussions.

Operational middle ground

Managed KMS and centralized secrets platforms improve access control, rotation, and auditability. The tradeoff is that organizations often create fragmented control planes across clouds and platforms.

High-control model

Customer-controlled HSMs offer the strongest custody story. Private keys can remain in tamper-resistant hardware while applications use provider-backed handles and perform operations through the HSM boundary.

Best practice direction A Java keystore is usually better than a loose plaintext key file, but it is still normally a software container unless backed by an HSM provider. For long-lived, high-value keys, custody often matters as much as format.
Moving from file-based storage toward HSM-backed custody is usually a story about stronger control boundaries, better audit posture, and less secret sprawl.
Page 4 - Migration Strategy

Wrapping Up: What a Good ML-KEM Migration Strategy Looks Like

Legacy software patterns often store raw key material, while stronger keystore and HSM models move toward provider-backed entries and better private-key custody.

Organizations do not need to replace everything at once. The smartest programs usually start by protecting the longest-lived data and the highest-value trust paths first. In many environments, that means moving the key establishment layer toward ML-KEM while tightening how private keys are generated, stored, and used.

A practical migration checklist

  • Inventory where RSA key transport still protects high-value content.
  • Prioritize systems vulnerable to harvest-now, decrypt-later exposure.
  • Adopt ML-KEM where public-key key establishment or key wrapping is the core requirement.
  • Plan separate workstreams for post-quantum signatures, certificates, and HSM/provider support.
  • Use migration as an opportunity to reduce private-key sprawl and strengthen custody controls.
The organizations that transition well will treat ML-KEM as more than an algorithm swap. It is a chance to modernize both cryptography and key custody at the same time.

This page intentionally centers ML-KEM for key establishment. Post-quantum signatures, certificate formats, and HSM integration are related workstreams, not automatic side effects.
